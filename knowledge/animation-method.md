# Impeccable-informed animation direction

This is a project-specific adaptation for original, code-generated explanatory video. It supplies design methods to the existing single Sol 6.1 / high creator; it is not a visual reference, storyboard, style preset, or separate approval stage. The narration, actual audio/alignment, current segment, restricted SDK and text-safe layout remain authoritative. Apply this guidance only when its content is frozen in the current work item's `animation_authoring_contract.design_method_guidance`.

## Before the first source

Understand what the audience must see changing, and choose a visual language that makes those relationships legible. Decide the dominant composition, type hierarchy, color roles, treatment of form/material/depth, and motion character together. Make choices specific to this narration; a different topic should not inherit the same picture with swapped labels. Keep a few useful continuity notes in the existing segment handoff, then implement the current segment. This is thinking within the creator's existing turn, not another document, questionnaire or checkpoint.

Translate the complete narration into visible events and shot organization. Decide what the viewer can learn from each action, comparison or result, and let the staging develop as that meaning advances. Recompose, change viewpoint, move between relevant subjects or return to an earlier subject when the explanation benefits. A recurring subject is useful when its developing actions carry the explanation; consider whether a further orbit, highlight or label develops attention, visual rhythm, form or understanding rather than simply repeating the same presentation. Choose the visual progression within the existing creative turn and handoff, without a separate storyboard deliverable or prescribed shot count.

Before implementing each current segment, work out its continuous visual development against the actual speech and frame range: the starting composition and object states, the sequence of actions or transformations, shifts of focal attention or viewpoint, the readable result, and how the ending leads into the next segment. Make these choices concrete enough to implement their timing and transitions; do not leave the middle as an unspecified hold after an entrance. Design within the same creator turn, and carry only useful timing and state decisions in the existing handoff. This is not a separate storyboard artifact, approval, fixed shot template or additional model call. Plan only the current segment in this detail; future segment source must still wait its turn.

Resolve the shared caption area's contrast and background treatment in this first design decision, before implementing the first segment. Carry it through the existing handoff so all segments start with the same treatment. The shared CaptionLayer supplies the actual aligned words; never draw duplicate captions in scene code.

## Composition and craft

- Give the viewer a clear focal subject and supporting elements. Use scale, proximity, overlap, contrast and negative space deliberately; equal boxes and equal gaps should reflect actual equivalence in the content. Let the subject occupy the available canvas while preserving the actual title/caption text.
- Draw recognizable, well-proportioned forms with purposeful internal detail. Geometry, diagrams, vector illustration and procedural 3D are valid media. Choose coherent edges, line weights, perspective, lighting and material behavior. Detail should clarify the subject or its character; random particles and floating symbols cannot supply missing content.
- Establish a small, legible type hierarchy using fonts available in the project, including proper Chinese glyphs. Distinguish title, labels and measurements through weight, size, placement and tone. Check actual long strings. Keep information understandable at phone viewing size and never compete with or duplicate the shared captions. Within this same implementation turn, check text baselines, font size and line height against the actual inner bounds of every card, template and label, including transformed or reused variants. Reserve space for the full price and copy rather than testing only an empty template. Derive highlight boxes from the target object’s actual position, scale and text bounds in its current coordinate system; a copied global coordinate must not silently point to a different object. Carry corrected shared geometry through the existing handoff. This is a source-level craft check, not a new checklist artifact, approval, model call or per-segment render; actual whole-film review remains necessary.
- Keep the caption backing compact and visually secondary: a clean, subtly rounded, dark plate around the actual two-line caption bounds, with modest padding. For the current revision2 caption bounds (x132/y1376/w756/h112), use x112/y1358/w796/h148, radius18 as the starting geometry; derive equivalent padding from other frozen layouts rather than changing their coordinates. Use a restrained dark tone from the film palette with enough contrast for the shared white text. Avoid broad feathered gradients, glow, heavy blur/shadow, or a near-full-width banner; do not expand the backing to occupy the whole lower composition. Plan moving subjects around the actual text and compact backing from the first segment, without shrinking the entire scene. Solve contrast within this treatment instead of adding a large dark patch after the whole-film render.
- Give colors stable jobs: subject identity, a relationship, a change of state, or atmosphere. Choose a palette and light/dark treatment from the content and intended tone. Use contrast to guide attention. Preserve category meaning across segments; color alone must not be the only way to distinguish a fact.
- Inspect habitual solutions before using them: repeated card grids, giant number plus tiny label, neon halos, decorative glass, gradients, technical-looking monospace and empty grids. Use a treatment when the content and chosen visual language justify it. Replacing one fashionable template with another does not create specificity; extra ornament does not create finish.

## Motion as explanation

Make motion reveal a process, relationship, comparison or consequence in the narration. Objects can change shape, position, assembly, scale, connection, state or viewpoint. Preserve an object's identity while the audience follows its transformation. Carry continuity through the visual language, the explained relationship and the object state needed for the next action; choose the subject and composition for the current meaning. Compose the entire movement, including what leads, what follows, the visible result and the next transition; do not spend the segment on an entrance followed by a static slide.

Each source segment is an implementation unit of at most 30 seconds. It can contain several shots or visual events, and a shot can continue across a source boundary. Choose shot timing and transitions from the explanation and actual speech; file boundaries do not set shot duration. Use a sustained scene when its action continues to reveal something, and vary staging when a different presentation makes the next meaning clear. Decorative cuts and arbitrary object substitutions do not supply missing explanatory development.

Time actions to the real speech and available frames. Allow readable holds when the viewer needs them, and maintain meaningful development as the explanation advances. Choose acceleration, deceleration, anticipation or settling from the depicted action; no universal easing curve or interface-duration table applies to a film. Camera movement, masks, light and depth can direct attention, explain spatial relationships, or enrich the viewing rhythm and visual character. Visual development need not introduce a new fact at every change: vary shot scale, viewpoint, composition, object performance, material response, lighting and depth when they fit the current content and chosen visual language. A coherent sequence can make a familiar subject engaging through these changes while its identity and meaning remain stable. Choose the mix and timing for this segment rather than using every device, cutting at fixed intervals, or maintaining constant motion. Avoid identical fade-and-rise reveals or unrelated perpetual movement as the sole source of dynamism.

## Continuity and review

Carry the chosen visual language and the prior object state needed by a continuing action into the next segment. Vary staging when the content changes, while retaining the identities and relationships the audience still needs to follow, color meanings, type roles and motion behavior. Each generation remains at most 30 seconds, by the same creator, with all segment source completed before unified rendering.

Within the existing review, examine the actual rendered movement and text safety, not only an attractive still or the source code. Follow the whole film's visual development as the narration adds meaning: assess what the staging, actions and results contribute beyond further labels or camera turns around a familiar subject. Check whether the focal subject reads clearly, changes explain the speech, forms look intentional and the sequence joins naturally. Fix concrete visible weaknesses, including prolonged repetition that leaves the next idea visually unexplained. These are model judgments, not a numeric score or a deterministic aesthetic gate.
